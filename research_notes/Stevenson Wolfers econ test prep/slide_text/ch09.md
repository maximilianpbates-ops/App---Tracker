# From Micro to Macro
Macroeconomics builds upon microeconomics!

Microeconomics focuses on individuals…
Your individual income.
The output your business produces.
Your spending, or the spending of your family, or your company.
Macroeconomics focuses on totals…
Total income in the whole country.
Total output produced by all businesses.
Total spending across all people, businesses, and the country’s government.
Do NOT think of micro and macro as two distinct halves of economics!

Gross domestic product: the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given year.

# A simple example
Suppose a country X produces 10 apples and 30 oranges. If the price of apple is $5 each and the price of orange is $10 each, what is the GDP of this country?
Answer: GDP=price of apple*quantity of apples+ price of orange*quantity of oranges
=5*10+10*30
 GDP= $350

# Exclude Economic Activity Outside Market
Does NOT include economic activity that occurs outside of markets.
If you wash your car yourself, your labor is not included in GDP. However, if you take your car to the car wash, then that service is counted as GDP.

# Gross Domestic Product (GDP) (3 of 5)
Gross domestic product: the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given year.
“…final goods and services…”  count only final goods and services, omitting intermediate goods.
The price of a final good incorporates all the contributions of the prior stages of production.
Thus, GDP does NOT count intermediate goods: goods and services that are used as inputs in the production of other products.
Car example: The new car you buy for $35,000 is considered a final product (you are the final user!). The $35,000 price includes the value of all the contributions that created that car:
Intermediate goods: the metal, wires, plastic, glass, the workers who processed and assembled those pieces, the truck driver who delivered the car to the sales lot, and the salesperson who sold the car to you.

# Understanding Intermediate Goods
Goods or services used as inputs in the production of other products.
Whether a product is an intermediate good or a final good depends on the use of the product.
When you consume a ripe mango directly, it is the final good.
When you use the ripe mango to sell a glass of mango lassi for some $, that mango is an intermediate good.

# Gross Domestic Product (GDP) (4 of 5)
Gross domestic product: the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given year.
“…produced…”  omit resale of already-purchased goods.
GDP does NOT count resale of existing finished goods.
Second-hand sales merely change ownership of goods that have already been produced and were previously counted in GDP.
Example: If you bought a used car, that purchase would not be counted in GDP.

# The Circular Flow (3 of 3)
Each flow of real resources is matched by an equal and opposite flow of money.
The circular flow shows:
The market value of total output  must be equal to total spending.
Total spending must equal total income.
Total output, total spending, and total income are all equal.

# Three Perspectives on GDP

1. Total Spending
2. Total Output
3. Total Income
Measure GDP by adding up every dollar of income earned.
Highlights where income is going, and who’s enjoying the fruits of all the economic activity.
Measure GDP by adding up every dollar of spending.
Highlights who’s doing the spending, and what they are buying.
Measure GDP by adding up every dollar’s worth of output produce.
Highlights what’s being made, and by whom. It helps map the structure of production.
These three measurements are all the same in theory, but they get different names because real-world measurements can differ due to different sources of imperfect data.

# Limitations of GDP (1 of 6)
Recall, GDP is the market value  of all goods and services.
But our values are not the same as market prices.
GDP counts your spending on a good, but your benefit is often much larger if you enjoy consumer surplus.
Consider the following:
Internet services like Google and Wikipedia are sold at a price of zero.
PewDiePie’s book of meme-like wisdom for $15.99 versus a literary classic for $7.99.

Limitations of GDP:
Prices are not values.
Nonmarket activities — including household production — are excluded.
The shadow economy is missing.
Environmental degradation isn’t counted.
Leisure doesn’t count.
GDP ignores distribution.

# Limitations of GDP (5 of 6)
GDP counts the benefit of work but omits the cost of work  less leisure!
Working extra hours means less time to spend with friends or family, relax, watch a movie, or take a leisurely walk.
United States versus France: Work-leisure trade-off
The United States has a 40-hour workweek, whereas France has a 35-hour workweek plus substantial paid vacation time, sick leave, and paid parental leave.
Typical French employee works 300–400 fewer hours in a year (10 fewer weeks!).
French GDP per person is 28% lower than that of the United States.

Limitations of GDP:

# Broken Window Hypothesis
Can we increase GDP by destroying goods produced last year?
Consider the following:
A boy breaks a shopkeeper’s window.
The shopkeeper than orders a new window.
This leads to production of a new window.
Will the GDP increase as a result?
Not really!
Production possibility frontier

# Nominal Vs. Real GDP
GDP values calculated for a year depends on the price values.
Suppose a country X produces 10 apples and 30 oranges. If the price of apple is $5 each and the price of orange is $10 each, what is the GDP of this country?
Answer: GDP=$350.
Suppose a country X produces 10 apples and 30 oranges. If the price of apple is $10 each and the price of orange is $20 each, what is the GDP of this country?
Answer: GDP=$700.
GDP is higher in the second case even the output is the same!
Inflation adjustment.
