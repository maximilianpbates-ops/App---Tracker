# Inflation
Inflation: a generalized rise in the overall level of prices.
Also described as…
A rise in the cost of living.
A decline in the purchasing power of money.
Important: Not every price change is a sign of inflation. Relative price adjustments happen as a good’s supply and demand shift.

# The price of a basket of goods and services
There is more than one measure of inflation.
Most relevant to your life as a consumer is the   measure calculated using the consumer price index.
Consumer price index (CPI): an index that tracks the average price consumers pay over time for a representative “basket” of goods and services.
“Basket” is a metaphor for the list of goods and services people typically buy.
It’s the food you buy, but also the shelter, education, haircuts, and streaming services you pay for.

# How to calculate the inflation rate
The inflation rate is the annual percentage increase in the average price level:

On average, prices are 4% higher this year than last year.
The cost of living is 4% higher than it was last year.
A dollar buys roughly 4% less than it did last year.
Example: If the price of the basket rose from $100 last year to $104 this year, then the inflation was 4%.

# Steps to Calculate Inflation
Find the basket of things people buy in a particular year (base year).
Collect prices of base year and another year.
Tally up the cost of the basket in both years.
Calculate inflation as the percentage change in the prices of the basket.

# Challenges of measuring the true cost of living (1 of 2)

CPI likely overstates changes in the cost of living because it tracks the changing price of a fixed basket of goods.
Consumers’ buying patterns change often!

Three things CPI misses:
Quality improvements
New products
Substitutions
1. Quality improvements can hide price decreases.
Measured rise in prices is likely due to unmeasured quality improvements.
Example: iPhones are now more expensive relative to the first model.
But the new iPhone has better storage, processing speed, battery life, camera, etc.
2. Doesn’t account for the reduction in the cost of living due to the introduction of new products.
iPhone replaced many goods and services on which people previously spent lots of money.
Replaced: landline, answering machines, calculators, cameras and film, GPS devices, music players, etc.

12

### Notes:
Note on the degree to which CPI overstates the cost of living:
Studies suggest that the bias due to the unmeasured quality improvements, new products, and substitution together lead the CPI to overstate the rising cost of living by nearly 1% per year.

Note on ”Quality Improvements”
iPhone details: A 128 GB iPhone 13 Pro cost $999 in 2022, which is a 66% price increase over the first iPhone.

Note on “New Products”
The BLS adds new products to the basket and removes others, but it doesn't compare the price of new products (like iPhones) to the products being replaced (like cameras).

# Nominal and Real Variables
Nominal variable: a variable measured in dollars (whose value may fluctuate over time).
Real variable: a variable that has been adjusted to account for inflation.

Real value in 2012 dollars = Nominal value in year t  dollars  ×
Intuition: Dividing the Nominal value in year t  dollars by the price level in year t gives the quantity in year t. Multiplying the price level in 2012 gives the real value in that year.

# Concept Check: Nominal to Real
In 2020, Target’s annual revenue was $78.1 billion.
In 2021, Target’s annual revenue increased to $93.5 billion.
The CPI in 2020 was 258.8, and the CPI in 2021 rose to 271.
Question: Calculate the growth in Target’s real revenue using 2012 as the base year, when CPI was 229.6.

Revenue from 2020 in 2012 dollars:         $78.1 b x (229.6/258.8) = $69 billion
Revenue from 2021 in 2012 dollars:           $93.5 b x (229.6/271) = $79 billion
Percentage change in revenue:                  ($79 b – $69 b)/$69 b = an increase of 14%

# Real and Nominal Interest Rates
Nominal interest rate: the stated interest rate without a correction for the effects of inflation.
Real interest rate: the interest rate in terms of changes in your purchasing power.
Real interest rate = Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate
The above formula only holds for low values of the inflation rate. However, use this formula if no other information is given.
18

<!-- Slide number: 19 -->
# Example
Example: Suppose you put $100 into your savings account. The bank will pay you 5% nominal interest at the end of the year. During this time, the inflation rate was 3%.
5% − 3% = 2%.  Saving $100 for a year made you $2 better off.

# Implications
The benefits and costs of saving or borrowing money are impacted by inflation.
If you are a borrower, higher inflation reduces your real cost of borrowing.
If you are a lender, higher inflation reduces your real return on lending.

# Money Illusion
Money Illusion: the (mistaken) tendency to focus on nominal dollar amounts instead of inflation-adjusted amounts.
Money illusion can….
Distort decisions.
What if all prices throughout the economy (including incomes) rose by 25%?
Lead to mis-pricing.
Make sure you correctly value the home that you bought a few years ago.
Create nominal wage rigidity.
Employers often choose to stick with last year’s wage.
Money illusion leads workers to feel okay, even though their real wage fell.

# The costs of inflation
Inflation undermines the productive benefit of money:
When inflation is low and stable, then money serves its three functions well.
High or unpredictable inflation erodes the functions of money.
Let’s examine the costs of different forms of inflation:

# The costs of hyperinflation

Hyperinflation: extremely high rates of inflation.
No precisely agreed upon point at which high inflation becomes classified as hyperinflation.
Hyperinflation is rare.
Examples:
German hyperinflation of 1922–1923 saw prices double every few days.
Venezuela is currently struggling with hyperinflation.
Hyperinflation can make life harder and erode all functions of money.
Venezuelan bolivar lost its value too quickly to function as a store of value or unit of account, and made it an unattractive medium of exchange.

# The costs of expected inflation (1 of 2)
Inflation goal of 2%
Inflation can stabilize at the same rate as people come to expect a certain level of inflation.
But even expected inflation can have costs.
Cost 1: Inflation creates menu costs for sellers.
Menu costs: the marginal cost of adjusting prices.
Businesses devote valuable resources to reprinting menus, adjusting price tags, and reprogramming vending machines more often.
Why?  inflation made money an unstable unit of account.
A higher price better covers the rising costs of your inputs this year.
The marginal benefit of raising your price exceeds the marginal cost of adjusting the price.

32

<!-- Slide number: 33 -->
# The costs of expected inflation (2 of 2)

Cost 2: Inflation creates shoe-leather costs for buyers.
Shoe-leather costs: the costs incurred trying to avoid holding cash.
Inflation undermines money’s function as a store of value.
People are spending time trying to keep their wealth in assets that better maintain their value.
As prices rise, the cash in your wallet (or in your Venmo or checking accounts) becomes worth less.
You don’t want to store your wealth in money (i.e., you don’t want to hold money).
This means spending money quickly, or trying to move money around, to preserve its value.

# The costs of unexpected inflation (1 of 2)

Cost 3: Inflation confuses the signals that prices send.
Prices help coordinate economic activity.
Quinoa example:
An increased price signals to producers that buyers want more quinoa.
Incentivizes producers to expand quinoa production.
But if inflation is causing all prices to rise, then there’s no reason to expand production.
Producers make mistakes interpreting price changes!
Inflation undermines the stability of dollars as a unit of account.
This instability confounds the signals that price sends.
34

<!-- Slide number: 35 -->
# The costs of unexpected inflation (2 of 2)

Cost 4: Inflation redistributes.
Unexpected inflation redistributes from savers and lenders toward borrowers.
Most loans specify repayment schedules in nominal terms.
Unexpected inflation changes the real value of the repayments.
Mortgage Example:
You buy a house, and you have fixed rate mortgage which requires you to pay $1,400 per month for the next 30 years.
If inflation is higher than expected, then you keep paying $1,400 per month even though the dollars you send the bank aren’t worth as much.
Hurts lenders and helps borrowers!

# Inflation Fallacy
Inflation fallacy: the mistaken belief that inflation destroys purchasing power.
Inflation means all prices  are rising, including the price of labor (i.e., wages and salaries).
If prices and wages rise together, then purchasing power is roughly unchanged.

A higher inflation  results in a lower purchasing power of a fixed dollar amount.
A $100 bill buys you fewer goods and services.

36

# Creates Psychological Bias

The inflation fallacy reflects a psychological bias:
People are quick to blame inflation for higher prices.
But interpret a rise in their nominal wages as an earned reward for their hard work.
