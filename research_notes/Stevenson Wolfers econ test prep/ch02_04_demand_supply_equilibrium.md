**Ch. 2 (Demand), from lecture slides "Based on Ch. 2 of 'Principles of Economics' by Betsey Stevenson & Justin Wolfers"** (M. Zweimüller, updated Nov 7, 2023). Source for every bullet in this block: [econ101_ch2_en.pdf](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/426761/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch2_en.pdf)
- Opening example: "What is Darren's individual demand curve?" Darren notices his tank is nearly empty, and the station sign says $3 per gallon. Axes: price in $/gallon (vertical) and gallons per week (horizontal).
- An individual demand curve "holds other things constant". **Law of demand**: "The quantity demanded is higher when the price is lower – holding other things constant!"
- **Rational Rule for Buyers**: "Buy more of an item if its marginal benefit is greater than (or equal to) the price." "Keep buying until price = marginal benefit." "The demand curve is also the marginal benefit curve."
- **Diminishing marginal benefit**: "Each additional item yields a smaller marginal benefit than the previous item" (ice-cream scoops example).
- **Market demand**: the sum of the quantity each person demands at each price. "Four Steps to Estimating Market Demand": (1) survey customers on the quantity they would buy at each price; (2) add up the total at each price; (3) scale up so the respondents represent the whole market; (4) plot.
- Market demand slopes down for two reasons: at low prices current customers buy more, and the number of customers rises.
- **Movement along the demand curve**: caused by a price change. **Change in quantity demanded**: the quantity change along a fixed curve. **Increase in demand**: rightward shift. **Decrease in demand**: leftward shift.
- **The six shifters.** Five shift both individual and market demand: "1. income. 2. preferences 3. prices of related goods. 4. expectations. 5. congestion and network effects." One shifts only market demand: "1. the type and number of buyers." The slides number them Demand Shifter 1 to 6 in that order.
- Definitions: normal good (higher income raises demand) and inferior good (higher income lowers demand). Complements, e.g. hot dogs and buns: demand falls when the complement's price rises. Substitutes, e.g. driving/walking/cycling/ride-sharing/bus: demand rises when the substitute's price rises. Expectations: if you expect prices to rise you buy today, increasing today's demand. **Network effect**: "a good becomes more useful because other people use it" (social media). **Congestion effect**: "a good becomes less valuable because other people use it" (roads).
- Rule of thumb: "When the price changes: you're thinking about a movement along the demand curve. When other factors change: you need to think about shifts."

**Ch. 3 (Supply), from S&W-based slides** (updated Nov 16, 2023). Source for every bullet in this block: [econ101_ch3_en.pdf](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414233/mod_resource/content/2/econ101_ch3_en.pdf)
- Opening example: "What is BP's individual supply curve? (How much gasoline is BP willing to supply at each price?)"
- **Law of supply**: "the tendency for the quantity supplied to be higher when the price is higher."
- **Perfectly competitive market**: all firms sell an identical good, with many buyers and sellers who are each small relative to the market. A **price-taker** is "an actor who charges the market price", and its actions "do not affect the market price."
- **Rational Rule for Sellers in Competitive Markets**: "Sell one more item if the price is greater than (or equal to) the marginal cost." Keep producing "until Price = Marginal cost". The supply curve is the marginal cost curve.
- "Marginal cost does include variable costs" (labor, raw materials) "but does not include fixed costs."
- The supply curve slopes up because of rising marginal cost, which comes from (1) "Diminishing marginal product" ("The marginal product of an input declines as you use more of that input") and (2) rising input costs.
- **Market supply** is the sum of the quantities supplied by each seller. Slide example: "The market contains 100 sellers. All sellers are identical. All sellers have the same supply schedule as BP."
- Market supply slopes up because a higher price leads each business to supply more and also brings more businesses into supplying.
- **The five shifters.** Four shift both individual and market supply: "1. input prices. 2. productivity and technology. 3. prices of related outputs. 4. expectations." One shifts only market supply: "1. the type and number of sellers."
- Definitions: **substitutes-in-production** are "Alternative uses of your production capacity". **Complements-in-production** are "Goods that are made together". Seller expectations: if you expect your product's price to rise next year, you store output and sell later, which "will decrease your supply this year." **Productivity growth**: producing more output with fewer inputs, often driven by technological change.
- In the Ch. 4 recap the third supply shifter is worded "Other opportunities and the prices of related outputs". — [econ101_ch4_en.pdf](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414243/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch4_en.pdf)

**Ch. 4 (Equilibrium), from S&W-based slides** (updated Nov 23, 2023). Source for every bullet in this block: [econ101_ch4_en.pdf](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414243/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch4_en.pdf)
- "A market is any setting that brings together potential buyers ('demanders') and potential sellers ('suppliers')." Markets determine what gets produced, how much, who produces it, who receives it, and at what price.
- "Equilibrium: Quantity supplied equals quantity demanded." In equilibrium, neither suppliers nor demanders have an incentive to change. Everyday Economics box: diamonds vs. water.
- **Three-Step Recipe**: (1) Is the supply curve or demand curve (or both) shifting? (2) Increase (right) or decrease (left)? (3) How will price and quantity change at the new equilibrium?
- Worked examples, all matching the instructor's list:
  - A retailer installs EV charging stations (400 spaces in 120 cities): demand for EVs shifts right, so P and Q rise.
  - Amazon develops 30-minute delivery: demand at local brick-and-mortar stores shifts left, so P and Q fall.
  - Federal funding for cheaper hybrid-car batteries: supply shifts right, so P falls and Q rises.
  - California drought raises water costs for water-intensive almonds: supply shifts left, so P rises and Q falls.
- Double-shift example (gasoline): a recession cuts incomes (demand shifts left) while crude-oil prices rise (supply shifts left). "When supply and demand both shift, the conclusion will often be 'it depends' … The total effect depends on which curve shifts the most."
- **Interpreting Market Data, "two key rules"**: "1. If prices and quantities move in the same direction, then the demand curve has definitely shifted. (It's possible that supply may have also shifted.) 2. If prices and quantities move in opposite directions, then the supply curve has definitely shifted."
- Housing example: every summer the quantity of houses sold rises dramatically while price barely changes. Both demand (people prefer to move in summer) and supply (people buying a new house are also selling their old one) increase.
- **Typo in that slide deck**: Figure 3 says "When price is below equilibrium price => Quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded, and a shortage results." This is wrong. Below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. If the instructor's slides repeat it, it is a trap.

## Key Question 2: Real-world evidence for the slide examples

### Takeaway
Most slide examples hold up against data, though several need nuance. Walmart outperformed Target in 2008. Almond prices doubled during the 2012–16 drought while output fell (a supply shift), but in 2022 price and output fell together, which by the book's own diagnostic rule means demand shifted. Q1 2025 imports jumped 37.9% (annualized) ahead of tariffs, then fell about 30% in Q2. Toilet-paper sales rose about 213% in one week of March 2020 with no rise in consumption. Rose imports peak in February. Digitization cut book prices 10–15%.

**Network effects and congestion (demand shifter 5)**
- **Metcalfe's law** says a network's value is proportional to the square of the number of users (V ∝ n²). — [Wikipedia: Metcalfe's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metcalfe%27s_law)

- Van Hove (2016) adjusted the test for quality and cost and still found Metcalfe's law fit best. — [Van Hove 2016 (PDF)](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/metcalfes-law/2016-vanhove-2.pdf)
- A 2022 follow-up by the same group argues the newer data fit a **cube law** (V ∝ n³) better. — [Springer, JCST 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11390-022-2845-7)

### Inferences
**Walmart/Target.** The case fits "income → demand" as follows:
- A recession lowers incomes, so demand for goods that behave like inferior goods (discount staples) rises and demand for normal goods (Target's "cheap chic" discretionary items) falls.
- Strictly, "inferior" describes goods, not stores, so the clean framing is that the bundle Walmart sells behaves more like inferior goods.
- The 2009 reversal is a useful reminder that stock prices reflect expectations, not just current demand.

**Almonds through the book's own diagnostic rules.** Year pairs:
- **2011→2014**: P rose $1.99→$4.00 (+101%) while Q fell 2,030→1,870M lb (−8%). They moved in opposite directions, so **supply definitely shifted (left)**. This is consistent with drought and yield per acre falling 2,540→2,010.
- **2012→2013**: P rose (2.58→3.21) and Q rose (1,890→2,010). They moved in the same direction, so **demand definitely increased** in that year as well.
- **2021→2022**: P fell (1.86→1.40) and Q fell (2,935→2,580). They moved in the same direction, so **demand definitely shifted (left)**, even though drought was also cutting supply. This is consistent with the port and shipping disruptions and weaker export demand reported for 2022.
- So the slide's single-shift drought story (supply left, P up, Q down) fits 2011–14 well but not 2022, which makes a good "hard exam" case.

**Front-loading and toilet paper.** Both are textbook "expectations" demand shifts:
- Expecting a higher future price (a tariff, or a shortage) raises current demand, so current quantity jumps.
- Demand later falls below normal because purchases were pulled forward: Q2 2025 imports −30%.
- In the toilet-paper case, retail shelf prices were sticky, so the demand shift showed up as **shortages** (empty shelves) rather than higher prices. That is the Ch. 4 shortage concept in action.

**Valentine's roses.** February brings both the import peak (Q↑) and higher wholesale prices (P↑). Same direction means demand increased: a seasonal preference shift. Short-run supply is inelastic, so most of the adjustment shows up in price.

**Kindle.** There are two valid framings, and an exam could use either:
- (a) E-readers and e-books are **substitutes** for print books, so demand for print shifts left and print P and Q fall.
- (b) Digitization lowers the **marginal cost** of producing and distributing books, so supply shifts right, P falls, and Q (titles and units) rises. This is the Waldfogel–Reimers result.
- Which framing the slides intend could not be confirmed.

**Network vs. congestion effects.** Under Metcalfe's law, a platform's value to each user grows with the number of users, so each new adopter shifts others' demand right. Roads are the opposite case: congestion shifts demand left as more people use them.

## Key Question 3: Catalogue of calculation and graph-reasoning methods, with worked examples

### Takeaway
A hard exam on Ch. 2–4 can test ten distinct skills: (1) the discrete Rational Rule (MB ≥ P; P ≥ MC); (2) building market curves by horizontal summation, from tables, identical-buyer equations, and kinked sums; (3) survey scaling; (4) solving linear equilibrium; (5) shortage/surplus at a non-equilibrium price; (6) new equilibrium after a shift of a stated size; (7) double shifts with magnitudes; (8) consumer and producer surplus, discrete and triangle; (9) inferring which curve shifted from ΔP and ΔQ; (10) classifying shifters. The worked examples below are constructed for these notes; every number was checked with code.

### Cited Findings
- The Rational Rule for Buyers works unit by unit with a tie: buy if MB ≥ P. Slide practice: MB of coffee cups $4, $3, $2 at a price of $3 gives **2 cups**. With 100 identical classmates the market quantity demanded is **200 cups**. — [S&W-based Ch. 2 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/426761/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch2_en.pdf)
- The Rational Rule for Sellers works the same way: sell if P ≥ MC, and fixed costs are irrelevant. Slide practice: a shoe store with fixed costs $100 and MC of $10, $20, $30, at a price of $20, supplies **2 pairs**. With 100 identical stores the market supply is **200 pairs**. — [S&W-based Ch. 3 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414233/mod_resource/content/2/econ101_ch3_en.pdf)
- Law of supply check: a deli willing to sell 200 sandwiches at $10 will sell fewer at $8 (the slide's answer is 100). This tests direction only. — [S&W-based Ch. 3 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414233/mod_resource/content/2/econ101_ch3_en.pdf)
- Market demand is the sum of individual quantities at each price. Steps: survey, add, scale up, plot. — [Ch. 2 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/426761/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch2_en.pdf)
- The number of buyers changes market demand without shifting individual demand. — [Ch. 2 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/426761/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch2_en.pdf)
- The Three-Step Recipe and the two diagnostic rules apply to shift questions. With double shifts, "the total effect depends on which curve shifts the most." — [Ch. 4 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414243/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch4_en.pdf)
- GLS (the intermediate text) puts the calculus of equilibrium (Appendix 2A) and of consumer/producer surplus (Appendix 3A) in its Ch. 2–3. That is a hint that an instructor drawing on GLS would test algebraic equilibrium and surplus triangles. — [Macmillan GLS 4e TOC](https://www.macmillanlearning.com/college/us/product/Microeconomics/p/1319330576)

### Inferences

#### A. Formula sheet (standard algebra; derived)
- **Linear equilibrium**: with Qd = a − bP and Qs = c + dP, setting Qd = Qs gives **P\* = (a − c)/(b + d)** and **Q\* = (ad + bc)/(b + d)**.
- **Shortage / surplus at price P**: shortage = Qd(P) − Qs(P) when P < P\*; surplus = Qs(P) − Qd(P) when P > P\*. The amount actually traded = min(Qd, Qs). This is the "short side" rule.
- **Demand shifts horizontally by Δa** (e.g., "+20 units at every price"): ΔP\* = Δa/(b + d) and ΔQ\* = d·Δa/(b + d).
  - The matching **vertical** shift is Δa/b dollars. Price rises by **less** than the vertical shift unless supply is vertical.
- **Supply shifts horizontally by Δc**: ΔP\* = −Δc/(b + d) and ΔQ\* = b·Δc/(b + d).
- **Horizontal summation**:
  - With N identical buyers each with qᵢ = α − βP, market Q = N(α − βP).
  - Always sum **quantities at a given price** (Q as a function of P), never prices.
  - If you are given inverse demand P = α/β − q/β, first solve it for q.
- **Kinked market curves**: when buyers have different choke prices (or sellers different minimum prices), the market curve has a kink at each choke or entry price. Write it piecewise.
- **Consumer surplus**:
  - Discrete: CS = Σ (MBᵢ − P) over the units bought.
  - Linear: CS = ½ × Q\* × (choke price − P\*), where the choke price is the P at which Qd = 0.
- **Producer surplus**:
  - Discrete: PS = Σ (P − MCᵢ) over the units sold.
  - Linear: PS = ½ × Q\* × (P\* − supply's price intercept).
  - Fixed costs never enter MC, so they never change the quantity chosen.
- **Total expenditure (= sellers' revenue)** = P × Q. For example, gas at equilibrium: $3 × 2.0B = $6B per week.

#### B. Worked Example 1: the instructor's gasoline market (tables → equations → shift)
The tables fit straight lines exactly over $1–$5, with Q in billions of gallons per week:
- **Demand: Qd = 3.2 − 0.4P**. Check: P=5 gives 1.2; P=4 gives 1.6; P=3 gives 2.0; P=2 gives 2.4; P=1 gives 2.8.
- **Supply (100 × BP): Qs = 0.5 + 0.5P**. Check: P=1 gives 1.0; P=2 gives 1.5; P=3 gives 2.0; P=4 gives 2.5; P=5 gives 3.0.
- Caveat: below $1 BP's schedule drops to 0 at $0.50, so the supply line holds only for P ≥ $1.

Equilibrium: 3.2 − 0.4P = 0.5 + 0.5P, so 2.7 = 0.9P, giving **P\* = $3 and Q\* = 2.0B**. ✔ This matches the slides.

Disequilibrium:

| Price | Qd | Qs | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1 | 2.8 | 1.0 | shortage 1.8 |
| $2 | 2.4 | 1.5 | shortage 0.9 ✔ |
| $4 | 1.6 | 2.5 | surplus 0.9 ✔ |
| $5 | 1.2 | 3.0 | surplus 1.8 |

Shifts and double shifts:
- **Demand rises by 0.45B at every price** (Qd = 3.65 − 0.4P): 3.65 − 0.4P = 0.5 + 0.5P gives **P = $3.50, Q = 2.25B**.
- **Supply falls by 0.45B at every price** (Qs = 0.05 + 0.5P): **P = $3.50, Q = 1.80B**.
- **Both shifts together**: 3.65 − 0.4P = 0.05 + 0.5P gives **P = $4.00, Q = 2.05B**. Price rises for sure; quantity rises slightly.
- **Same demand rise, but supply falls by 0.9B** (Qs = −0.4 + 0.5P): **P = $4.50, Q = 1.85B**. Now quantity falls. This is the "ambiguous" cell in action: the direction of Q depends on which shift is larger.

Consumer surplus at equilibrium: the demand choke price is $8 (3.2/0.4), so CS = ½ × 2.0B × ($8 − $3) = **$5.0B per week**.

#### C. Worked Example 2: Darren, the discrete Rational Rule, and CS from a schedule
- Darren's schedule ($5→1, $4→2, $3→3, $2→5, $1→7 gallons) implies marginal benefits of gallon 1 = $5, gallon 2 = $4, gallon 3 = $3, gallons 4–5 = $2, and gallons 6–7 = $1.
- At P = $3 he buys 3 gallons, because MB₃ = $3 ≥ $3 and MB₄ = $2 < $3. CS = (5−3) + (4−3) + (3−3) = **$3**.
- At P = $2 he buys 5 gallons. CS = 3 + 2 + 1 + 0 + 0 = **$6**. The fall in price raises CS in two ways: by $1 on each of the 3 gallons he was already buying ($3), and by $0 on the 2 new marginal gallons, which are bought exactly at MB = P.
- **Deriving MB from total willingness to pay.** Suppose the total WTP for 1, 2, 3, 4 pizza slices is $8, $14, $18, $20. Then MB = $8, $6, $4, $2.
  - At P = $4 he buys **3 slices** (tie: buy).
  - CS = (8−4) + (6−4) + (4−4) = **$6**. Equivalently, total WTP $18 − spending $12 = $6.
  - The trap is comparing *total* WTP with price.

#### D. Worked Example 3: horizontal summation with equations and kinks
- **N identical buyers**: qᵢ = 10 − 2P with N = 100 gives **Q = 1,000 − 200P**.
  - Wrong method: summing the inverse demands P = 5 − q/2 "vertically" to get P = 500 − 50q. That treats the good as non-rival.
- **Different buyers**: A has qA = 10 − 2P (choke $5) and B has qB = 6 − P (choke $6).
  - Market demand: **Q = 6 − P for $5 ≤ P ≤ $6**, and **Q = 16 − 3P for P < $5**. The curve has a kink at P = $5, where Q = 1.
  - At P = $3: qA = 4 and qB = 3, so Q = 7. Check: 16 − 9 = 7.
- **Supply with an entry price**: Firm X has qX = 2P − 4 for P ≥ $2 (0 otherwise) and Firm Y has qY = P.
  - Market supply: **Q = P for P < $2**, and **Q = 3P − 4 for P ≥ $2**.
  - This illustrates the slide point that higher prices bring more sellers into the market.
- **Survey scaling**: 300 respondents represent 300 million people, a scale factor of 1,000,000. If respondents together demand 2,000 gallons at $3, the market demands 2.0B gallons.

#### E. Worked Example 4: a generic linear market (equilibrium, disequilibrium, shift, surplus)
Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = −20 + 4P.
- **Equilibrium**: 100 − 2P = −20 + 4P gives **P\* = 20, Q\* = 60**. The formula check works: (100 + 20)/6 = 20, and (100·4 + 2·(−20))/6 = 60.
- **Disequilibrium**:
  - At P = 15: Qd = 70 and Qs = 40, a shortage of 30, so 40 units are traded.
  - At P = 25: Qd = 50 and Qs = 80, a surplus of 30, so 50 units are traded.
- **Surplus**: the choke price is 50 and the supply intercept is P = 5. CS = ½·60·(50 − 20) = **900**. PS = ½·60·(20 − 5) = **450**.
- **Demand rises by 30 units at every price** (Qd = 130 − 2P): **P = 25, Q = 80**.
  - Formula check: ΔP = 30/(2 + 4) = 5 and ΔQ = 4·5 = 20.
  - The vertical shift is 30/2 = $15, yet price rises only $5.
  - New CS = ½·80·(65 − 25) = **1,600**. New PS = ½·80·(25 − 5) = **800**.

#### F. Worked Example 5: producer side and fixed costs
- Suppose MC of units 1–5 is $2, $3, $4, $5, $6. At P = $5 the firm sells **4 units** (tie at MC₄ = $5: sell).
- PS = 3 + 2 + 1 + 0 = **$6**.
- Adding a $10 fixed cost changes **neither** the quantity nor the MC schedule. This is the slides' shoe-store logic.
- If P rises to $6, the firm moves along its supply curve to 5 units. That is a change in quantity supplied, **not** an "increase in supply" (the slides' burger question).

#### G. Double-shift table and diagnostic logic (S&W Ch. 4)

| Shifts | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| D↑ and S↑ | ? | ↑ |
| D↓ and S↓ | ? | ↓ |
| D↑ and S↓ | ↑ | ? |
| D↓ and S↑ | ↓ | ? |

- **Reverse inference** (from observed price and quantity changes):
  - P and Q move the same way: demand definitely shifted.
  - They move in opposite ways: supply definitely shifted.
  - P is unchanged while Q rises: both curves increased (houses in summer).
  - Q is unchanged while P rises: demand rose and supply fell, with offsetting quantity effects.
- Knowing only one of ΔP or ΔQ is not enough to identify the shift (Roberts mistake #3 below).
- **Shifter direction drills**:

| Change | Shift |
|---|---|
| Price of a **complement** (gas) ↑ | demand for cars ← |
| Price of a **substitute** ↑ | demand → |
| Income ↑ (normal good) | demand → |
| Income ↑ (inferior good) | demand ← |
| Buyers expect a higher future price | demand today → |
| **Sellers** expect a higher future price | supply today ← |
| Price of beef ↑ | supply of **leather** → (complement-in-production) |
| Price of wheat ↑ | supply of **corn** ← (substitute-in-production) |
| Input price ↑ | supply ← |
| Productivity ↑ | supply → |
| More sellers | market supply → |
| More users of a network good | demand → |
| More drivers on a road | demand for that road ← (congestion) |

  - The two expectation rows share a nuance: both raise today's price.

## Key Question 4: Common student traps and misconceptions (economics-education evidence)

### Takeaway
The best-documented errors are: (1) confusing a change in demand with a change in quantity demanded; (2) the circular "feedback" story (demand ↑ → price ↑ → demand ↓); (3) treating a price rise as a supply shift; (4) inferring the shifting curve from quantity alone; (5) confusing willingness to pay with what buyers must pay. Research on first-year students shows such preconceptions persist even among students who score well on tests.

### Cited Findings
- Russ Roberts, "Common mistakes using supply and demand", lists four mistakes. Source for the four mistakes below: [russroberts.info](https://russroberts.info/classroom-resources/common-mistakes-using-supply-and-demand/)
  - **Mistake 1** (movement vs. shift; circular reasoning): "income falls → demand for apples falls → price falls → demand rises → price back up → ambiguous." Correct reasoning: lower demand creates excess supply, price falls, and the lower price raises *quantity demanded* along the new curve. Fewer apples sell at a lower price. Quote: "changes in price don't shift the demand curve, they are movements along the demand curve."
  - **Mistake 2** (a supply shift treated as a demand shift): "sweetener cost ↑ → soda supply ↓ → price ↑ → buyers cut demand → price ↓." Correct: "The increase in price decreases quantity demanded, it doesn't decrease demand." Higher price, fewer sodas.
  - **Mistake 3** (inferring from quantity alone): "more houses built ⇒ supply rose ⇒ prices fall" is invalid, because more building can come from higher demand (e.g., firms relocating to the city), with prices rising. "An increase in the observed quantity of a good tells you that either demand or supply has shifted."
  - **Mistake 4** (willingness vs. obligation to pay): "Exxon's oil spill raises its costs → it raises prices → consumers pay more" is wrong if market supply doesn't change. Competitors and substitutes discipline the price, and "just because they are willing to pay more, they don't have to." Nuance: for a storable good, expectations of future scarcity can raise today's price.
- Saying a higher price "increases supply" wrongly implies a shift. A higher price normally causes an increase in quantity supplied along an unchanged curve. — [RevisionDojo (IB teacher resource)](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/ib-economics-demand-supply-common-mistakes)
- The S&W-based slides test exactly this trap. A burger price rise from $15 to $18 means "Your quantity supplied of burgers increases", not a shift. They also test that "an increase in the current price" decreases quantity demanded (a movement), unlike changes in a complement's price, the number of buyers, or expected future prices. — [Ch. 3 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414233/mod_resource/content/2/econ101_ch3_en.pdf); [Ch. 2 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/426761/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch2_en.pdf)
- Classification traps the slides test:
  - "car and gas" are NOT substitutes; they are complements.
  - Only "the number of buyers" changes market demand without shifting individual demand.
  - "The profit of a substitute-in-production rises" *decreases* supply, while falling input prices increase it.
  - Source: [Ch. 2 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/426761/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch2_en.pdf); [Ch. 3 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414233/mod_resource/content/2/econ101_ch3_en.pdf)
- Busom, Lopez-Mayan & Panadés (2017), "Students' persistent preconceptions and learning economic principles", *Journal of Economic Education* 48(2):74–92. They surveyed first-year principles students at the start and end of a semester and found "evidence of preconception persistence and reasoning inconsistencies", plausibly due to cognitive biases (including self-serving bias). A working version adds that most students do not integrate the new tools into their thinking "even if they perform well in tests." — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/jeduce/v48y2017i2p74-92.html); [Barcelona School of Economics summary](https://bse.eu/research/publications/students-persistent-preconceptions-and-learning-economic-principles)
- Teacher resources identify confusing quantity demanded with demand as "the most persistent misconception", along with misclassifying substitutes and complements. — [search-surfaced teacher resources, e.g. RevisionDojo](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/ib-economics-demand-supply-common-mistakes)

### Inferences
These are additional traps a hard exam is likely to exploit. Each is derived from the S&W slide logic above.
1. **"Increase in supply" looks like a downward shift.** A rightward supply shift also sits lower on the graph, and a decrease (leftward) sits higher. Students who read "up = increase" draw the wrong curve. Always think "more quantity at every price" (right) or "lower MC at every quantity" (down).
2. **Shortage vs. surplus labels.** P < P\* gives Qd > Qs, a **shortage**. P > P\* gives Qs > Qd, a **surplus**. One S&W-based slide deck states the shortage case backwards. Check the instructor's version.
3. **Buyers' expectations vs. sellers' expectations** move today's curves in opposite *curve* directions (demand right, supply left), yet both raise today's price.
4. **Complements-in-production vs. complements-in-consumption.** A higher beef price raises the *supply* of leather (produced jointly). It does not change the *demand* for leather.
5. **Fixed costs.** They do not affect MC, so they do not affect the quantity supplied (the shoe-store example). A rise in rent does not shift the short-run supply curve in the S&W framework.
6. **Individual vs. market shifters.** "Type and number of buyers/sellers" shifts only the *market* curve. An exam may ask which change leaves Darren's own curve unchanged.
7. **Horizontal, not vertical, summation.** Add quantities at each price. Kinks appear where a new buyer or seller enters.
8. **Discrete tie-breaking.** With MB = P exactly, the Rational Rule says buy ("greater than or equal to"). The slides' coffee (2 cups) and shoe (2 pairs) answers depend on this.
9. **Magnitude vs. direction in double shifts.** Only one of P or Q is determined. Exams that give magnitudes ("demand rises by 0.45B, supply falls by 0.9B") expect the student to compute the answer, not say "ambiguous".
10. **Real-data double shifts.** Almonds in 2022 (drought, yet P and Q both fell) show that the single-shift textbook story can mislead, and that the diagnostic rule ("same direction ⇒ demand definitely shifted") still applies.
