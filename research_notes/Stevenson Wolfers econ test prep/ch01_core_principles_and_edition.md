# Textbook Identity/Edition and Chapter 1 "The (Four) Core Principles of Economics": Validation and Enrichment

> **HEADLINE CORRECTION FOR THE REPORT WRITER:** The book behind these slides is almost certainly **NOT** Goolsbee/Levitt/Syverson (GLS). It is **Betsey Stevenson & Justin Wolfers, *Principles of Macroeconomics*** (Worth / Macmillan Learning), most likely the **2nd edition (©2023)**. The "four core principles" framework (Cost-Benefit, Opportunity Cost, Marginal, Interdependence), the "Rational Rule," the "Or what?" trick, and chapter titles like "Equilibrium: Where Supply Meets Demand" all belong to Stevenson & Wolfers. GLS writes an *intermediate* micro text with calculus appendices. Evidence is below.

## Q2. How does the book define the Chapter 1 terms?

### Takeaway
The S&W Chapter 1 definitions were confirmed verbatim via a university lecture deck explicitly built on S&W Ch. 1. One caveat: the deck uses the 1st-edition chapter title, so 2nd-edition wording may differ slightly.

### Cited Findings
All items below are from the [Zweimüller S&W Ch. 1 lecture slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414211/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch1_en.pdf) unless noted.

**Economics**
- "Economics is the study of people in the ordinary business of life."
- **Microeconomics** is "the study of individual decision making and the implications for specific markets."
- **Macroeconomics** is "the study of decision making across the whole economy."

**Cost-Benefit Principle**
- "Costs and benefits are the incentives that shape decisions. You should evaluate the full set of costs and benefits of any choice and only pursue those whose benefits are at least as large as their costs."
- **Willingness to pay** is the "economist trick" of converting each cost and benefit into a monetary equivalent: "what is the most that you would be willing to pay to obtain a particular benefit or avoid a particular cost?"
- "The amount you are willing to pay depends on how much you like coffee, not the price."
- Coffee example: willingness to pay $4 vs. price $3, so buy.
- Slide heading: **"Money Is the Measuring Stick, Not the Objective."** Money is "a common measuring stick" that lets you account for nonfinancial issues "such as satisfaction or time."
- **Economic surplus** is "the total benefits minus the total costs flowing from a decision. It is a measure of how much your decision has improved your well-being." Slide heading: "Maximize Your Economic Surplus."
- **Framing** "refers to how different alternatives are described or framed. Framing effects can lead you astray and can make identical choices seem different." Slide heading: "Focus on the Costs and Benefits, Not How They're Framed."

**Opportunity Cost Principle**
- "The true cost of something is the next best alternative you must give up to get it. Your decisions should reflect this opportunity cost, rather than just the out-of-pocket financial costs."
- "When economists say 'costs', they really mean opportunity costs."
- **Scarcity:** "The opportunity cost arises because of a fundamental economic problem: scarcity. Scarcity occurs because resources are limited… Therefore, all choices require a trade-off!"
- **Calculating opportunity cost.** Ask two questions: (1) What happens if you pursue your choice? (2) What happens under your next best alternative?
- **"Four Important Lessons About Opportunity Costs":**
  1. Some out-of-pocket costs are opportunity costs, such as MBA tuition.
  2. Opportunity costs don't need to involve out-of-pocket financial costs.
  3. Not all out-of-pocket costs are real opportunity costs.
  4. Some nonfinancial costs are not opportunity costs.
- **The "Or What?" trick:** "To apply the opportunity cost principle correctly, ask yourself 'or what?'… Asking 'or what?' forces you to consider the alternatives."
- **Sunk cost:** "A sunk cost is a cost that has been incurred and cannot be reversed. A sunk cost exists whether you make your choice or not, so it is not an opportunity cost. When weighing costs and benefits, a good decision maker ignores sunk costs." Slide heading: "You Should Ignore Sunk Costs."

**Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)**
- "The PPF illustrates the trade-offs you experience when deciding how to allocate scarce resources." Scarce resources include "time, money, raw inputs, and production capacity." It illustrates "the different sets of output that are attainable with scarce resources."
- Slide heading: "Moving Along Your PPF Reveals Your Opportunity Costs." "When you're on your PPF, you can't produce more of one output unless you produce less of another."
- Points inside the PPF are possible but "an inefficient use of your resources." Points outside are reachable "Only if you change something!" Slide heading: "Productivity Gains Shift Your PPF Outward."

**Marginal Principle**
- "Decisions about quantities are best made incrementally. You should break 'how many' decisions down into a series of smaller or marginal decisions."
- **Marginal benefit:** "The extra benefit from one unit."
- **Marginal cost:** "The extra cost from one extra unit."
- The marginal principle is useful for "how many" decisions but not "either/or" choices. The cost-benefit principle handles "either/or." "Apply the marginal principle first, then the cost-benefit principle."
- **The Rational Rule:** "If something is worth doing, keep doing it until your marginal benefits equals your marginal costs." Following it "maximizes economic surplus."

**Interdependence Principle**
- "Your best choice depends on your other choices, the choices others make, developments in other markets, and expectations about the future. When any of these factors change, your best choice might change."
- **"The Four Types of Interdependencies":**
  1. Dependencies between each of your individual choices
  2. Dependencies between people or businesses in the same market
  3. Dependencies between markets
  4. Dependencies through time
- Worked example (choosing classes):
  - *Your choices:* an econ class blocks others at the same time.
  - *Others' choices:* another student takes the last spot.
  - *Other markets:* econ skills become more valuable in the labor market.
  - *Future choices:* prerequisites met today shape future courses.

**Four-step process (MCOI)**
1. Marginal principle: break "how many" into marginal choices.
2. Cost-benefit: does marginal benefit exceed marginal cost?
3. Opportunity cost: evaluate all relevant costs and ask "or what?"
4. Interdependence: how might changes in other factors change the decision?

**Memory trick (four questions)**
- "One more?" (marginal)
- "Benefit beat cost?" (cost-benefit)
- "Or what?" (opportunity cost)
- "What else?" (interdependence)

**Secondary confirmation**
- Student study notes reproduce the same Cost-Benefit and Interdependence wording and the "Or what?" framing. — [CliffsNotes study note](https://www.cliffsnotes.com/study-notes/22859071); [Brainscape "Core Principles of Economics" flashcards](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/core-principles-of-economics-9367720/packs/16538913)

### Inferences
- The test will probably use these exact phrasings: "economic surplus," "willingness to pay," "Rational Rule," "Or what?", "sunk cost," "four types of interdependence," "how many vs. either/or."
- MC questions often test fine distinctions:
  - surplus is maximized where **MB = MC**, not where total benefit = total cost;
  - sunk costs are **not** opportunity costs;
  - room and board you would pay anyway is **not** an opportunity cost of school.

## Q3. The CEO "6,000 workers" framing question and Tversky & Kahneman (1981)

### Takeaway
The S&W CEO/insurance-company problem is a near-exact isomorph of Tversky & Kahneman's (1981) "Asian disease" problem:
- 600 lives become 6,000 jobs;
- "save 200 for sure" becomes "save 2,000 for sure";
- "1/3 chance save all, 2/3 none" stays the same.

In the original, 72% chose the sure option in the gain ("saved") frame, and 78% chose the gamble in the loss ("die") frame. Expected values were identical.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation.** Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1981). "The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice." *Science*, 211(4481), 453–458 (Jan. 30, 1981). DOI 10.1126/science.7455683. — [PDF of original (NCSU course mirror)](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/Social/f24/wrap/readings/Tversky+Kahneman-framing-1981.pdf); [Fermat's Library annotated copy](https://fermatslibrary.com/s/the-framing-of-decisions-and-the-psychology-of-choice)
- **Problem 1, gain frame [N = 152].** "Imagine that the U.S. is preparing for the outbreak of an unusual Asian disease, which is expected to kill 600 people…"
  - "If Program A is adopted, 200 people will be saved. [72 percent]"
  - "If Program B is adopted, there is 1/3 probability that 600 people will be saved, and 2/3 probability that no people will be saved. [28 percent]"

  — [Original PDF](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/Social/f24/wrap/readings/Tversky+Kahneman-framing-1981.pdf)
- **Problem 2, loss frame [N = 155].**
  - "If Program C is adopted 400 people will die. [22 percent]"
  - "If Program D is adopted there is 1/3 probability that nobody will die, and 2/3 probability that 600 people will die. [78 percent]"

  The authors write: "The majority choice in problem 2 is risk taking." — [Original PDF](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/Social/f24/wrap/readings/Tversky+Kahneman-framing-1981.pdf)
- **The paper's main claims:**
  - preferences reverse when the same problem is framed differently;
  - "choices involving gains are often risk averse and choices involving losses are often risk taking."

  — [Original PDF](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/Social/f24/wrap/readings/Tversky+Kahneman-framing-1981.pdf); [Wikipedia: Framing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framing_effect_(psychology))
- **The S&W version (from the S&W-based deck):**
  - "You are the CEO of a large but struggling insurance company… You anticipate needing to fire 6,000 of your employees…"
  - "Plan A Saves 2,000 jobs."
  - "Plan B Has a one-in-three chance of saving all 6,000 jobs, but a two-in-three chance of saving no jobs at all."
  - Follow-up slide reframes: "Remember: If you [do] nothing, your firm will lose 6,000 jobs."

  — [Zweimüller S&W Ch. 1 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414211/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch1_en.pdf)
- **Bonus: mental-accounting problems in the same paper, useful for a reading on opportunity cost and sunk cost:**
  - **Problem 8 [N = 183]: lost a $10 bill** on the way to a $10 play. 88% would still buy a ticket; 12% would not.
  - **Problem 9 [N = 200]: lost the $10 ticket itself.** Only 46% would pay $10 for another; 54% would not. The economics are identical, since both are a $10 loss.
  - **Problem 10:** 68% would drive 20 minutes to save $5 on a $15 calculator. Only 29% would do so when the calculator cost $125.
  - On sunk costs, the paper says: "a sunk-cost effect arises when a decision is referred to an existing account in which the current balance is negative."

  — [Original PDF](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/Social/f24/wrap/readings/Tversky+Kahneman-framing-1981.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Expected value check for the slide version:**
  - Plan A = 2,000 jobs saved for certain.
  - Plan B = (1/3)(6,000) + (2/3)(0) = 2,000 jobs expected.
  - The loss-frame equivalents are Plan A = "4,000 jobs lost for sure" and Plan B = "1/3 chance no one loses a job, 2/3 chance all 6,000 do."
  - A rational chooser following the cost-benefit principle should not flip between frames. The textbook's lesson: focus on the costs and benefits, not the description.
- The 72%/78% figures are often misreported. 72% chose the **sure** option in the "saved" frame; 78% chose the **gamble** in the "die" frame. They come from different samples (N = 152 and N = 155).
- The $5-discount framing (save $5 on $15 vs. $125) is a strong illustration of the S&W point that a dollar saved is a dollar saved, regardless of the percentage framing.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether the S&W book itself cites Tversky & Kahneman (1981) by name next to the CEO problem.

## Q4. Citable research on sunk cost fallacy and opportunity cost neglect

### Takeaway
Three classic findings support Chapter 1's warnings:
- Arkes & Blumer (1985) showed sunk costs distort choices in surveys and in a randomized field experiment, and economics coursework did not help.
- Staw (1976) launched the escalation-of-commitment literature.
- Frederick et al. (2009) showed people fail to consider opportunity costs unless prompted, which is exactly what the "Or what?" trick does.

### Cited Findings
**Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). "The Psychology of Sunk Cost." *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 35, 124–140.**
- Definition: "The sunk cost effect is manifested in a greater tendency to continue an endeavor once an investment in money, effort, or time has been made." The authors attribute it to "the desire not to appear wasteful." — [Arkes & Blumer 1985 (PDF)](https://cognition.aau.at/bg/BA/Arkes%20%26%20Blumer%201985.pdf)
- **Ski trip (Experiment 1).** Subjects had spent $100 on a Michigan ski trip and $50 on a Wisconsin trip they expected to enjoy *more*; both fell on the same weekend.
  - 33 chose the $100 Michigan trip and 28 chose Wisconsin.
  - "only 46% of the subjects chose the Wisconsin trip," vs. 100% predicted by "traditional economic theory."

  — [Arkes & Blumer 1985](https://cognition.aau.at/bg/BA/Arkes%20%26%20Blumer%201985.pdf)
- **Theater field experiment (Experiment 2).** Season-ticket buyers for the Ohio University Theater's 1982–83 season were randomly sold full-price ($15), $2-discount, or $7-discount tickets.
  - In the first half of the season, the full-price group attended 4.11 plays vs. 3.32 and 3.29 for the discount groups (p < .05, one-tailed).
  - There was no significant difference in the second half.

  — [Arkes & Blumer 1985](https://cognition.aau.at/bg/BA/Arkes%20%26%20Blumer%201985.pdf)
- **Radar-blank plane (Experiment 3).**
  - With $10M already invested and the project 90% complete (and a competitor's plane already on the market, faster and cheaper), 41 of 48 (≈85%) said finish it.
  - With nothing invested yet, only 10 of 60 (≈17%) said build it.
  - χ²(1, N = 108) = 50.6, p < .001. This resolves the conflicting "48 vs 108 subjects" reports in secondary sources: 48 + 60 = 108.

  — [Arkes & Blumer 1985](https://cognition.aau.at/bg/BA/Arkes%20%26%20Blumer%201985.pdf)
- **Economics training didn't help.** "The sunk cost effect was not lessened by having taken prior courses in economics." Students currently in an economics course were tested as well. — [Arkes & Blumer 1985](https://cognition.aau.at/bg/BA/Arkes%20%26%20Blumer%201985.pdf)
- **Real-world hook.** The paper opens with senators defending continued funding of the Tennessee-Tombigbee Waterway on sunk-cost grounds ("To terminate a project in which $1.1 billion has been invested represents an unconscionable mishandling of taxpayers' dollars"). — [Arkes & Blumer 1985](https://cognition.aau.at/bg/BA/Arkes%20%26%20Blumer%201985.pdf)

**Staw (1976)**
- Citation: Staw, B. M. (1976). "Knee-Deep in the Big Muddy: A Study of Escalating Commitment to a Chosen Course of Action." *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance*, 16(1), 27–44. DOI 10.1016/0030-5073(76)90005-2. — [Wikipedia: Escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment)
- Finding: people "committed the greatest amount of resources to a previously chosen course of action when they were personally responsible for negative consequences." The study reportedly used a business-school simulation with ~240 students. — [Wikipedia: Escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment); [Gresham College lecture page](https://www.gresham.ac.uk/node/12941)

**Frederick, Novemsky, Wang, Dhar & Nowlis (2009). "Opportunity Cost Neglect." *Journal of Consumer Research*, 36(4), 553–561.** — [RePEc/IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v36y2009i4p553-561.html); [WashU profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/opportunity-cost-neglect/)
- **Origin anecdote.** One author was choosing between a $1,000 stereo and a $700 model. When the salesman asked whether he'd rather have the better system or "the cheaper system and $300 worth of CDs," he immediately chose the cheaper stereo. — [Yale SOM news](https://som.yale.edu/news/2009/12/another-shopping-tip-consider-opportunity-costs)
- **DVD experiment.** Interest in paying ~$15 for a DVD fell by about 20% when "not buy" was described as "keeping the money for other purchases." — [ScienceDaily, May 12, 2009](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/05/090512102558.htm)
- **iPod experiment.** The choice share of the cheaper 16 GB iPod Touch "roughly doubled" when the price difference was framed as "leaving you $100 in cash." — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/05/090512102558.htm)
- **Core claim.** Cues to consider opportunity costs reduce purchase rates and raise the choice share of cheaper options. — [Yale SOM news, May 2009](https://som.yale.edu/news/2009/05/opportunity-costs-remind-consumers-about-savings)

### Inferences
- The S&W "Or what?" trick is essentially the experimental manipulation in Frederick et al. Making the forgone alternative explicit changes choices. This is a strong "why this principle matters" hook for the reading.
- **Sunk-cost exam traps.** The deck's practice items show the textbook's style: the $13 movie you hate (leave; $13 is sunk), the $35 Halloween costume when sick, and the $700 Puerto Rico package vs. a free Miami trip with friends. — [Zweimüller S&W Ch. 1 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414211/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch1_en.pdf)
  - The book's answer pattern: the past payment is sunk, so compare only future benefits and costs.
  - Arkes & Blumer show most people (even econ students) get this wrong.

## Q5. Real-world evidence: movies in the 2008 recession, streaming in the pandemic, the opportunity cost of college

## Q6. Calculation methods for Chapter 1 (surplus, PPF, MB/MC tables), with verified arithmetic

### Takeaway
The restaurant table in the slides checks out:
- Profit (an *economic* profit, since the owner's $1,000 opportunity cost is counted) is $1,200 at both 5 and 6 workers.
- The 6th worker has MB = MC = $500, so the owner is indifferent about hiring that worker.
- The 7th worker has MB $250 < MC $400, so don't hire.

### Cited Findings
**Economic surplus**
- Economic surplus = total benefits − total costs. — [Zweimüller S&W Ch. 1 slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414211/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch1_en.pdf)
- Textbook coffee example: WTP $4 vs. price $3, so buy. Surplus = $1.
- Practice item: WTP $15 vs. price $16, so don't buy.

  — [Zweimüller slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414211/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch1_en.pdf)

**PPF math**
- Textbook example: "The opportunity cost of earning 4 more points in psychology is earning 8 fewer points in economics." — [Zweimüller slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414211/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch1_en.pdf)
- General PPF rules (OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e*, §2.2) — [OpenStax](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/2-2-the-production-possibilities-frontier-and-social-choices):
  - "the slope of the production possibilities frontier shows the opportunity cost";
  - the bowed-out shape reflects "the law of increasing opportunity cost" (diminishing returns), while a straight line implies constant relative cost;
  - points on the frontier are productively efficient;
  - "any choice inside the production possibilities frontier is productively inefficient and wasteful";
  - points outside are unattainable;
  - more resources or better technology shift the PPF outward.

**MB/MC hiring table: verified by recomputation (Python)**

| 3 | 210 | $5,250 | $4,500 | $750 | 50 | $1,250 | $800 |

**Other worked examples, recomputed from the deck's stated inputs** (the deck's result tables are images, so the book's own totals were not visible) — [Zweimüller slides](https://gwb.schule.at/pluginfile.php/414211/mod_resource/content/1/econ101_ch1_en.pdf)
- **Nerida: car vs. Uber.** Inputs: car $10,000, resale $8,000 after a year; work 5 miles away; 25 mpg; 50 work weeks; gas $3/gal; insurance $1,500/yr; repairs $500/yr; parking $5/day; Uber $10/ride.
  - Assuming 5 workdays/week (250 days), the car costs:
    - $2,000 depreciation
    - $300 gas (2,500 miles ÷ 25 mpg × $3)
    - $1,500 insurance
    - $500 repairs
    - $1,250 parking
    - **total $5,550**
  - Uber costs 500 rides × $10 = **$5,000**.
  - On pure dollars, Uber is $550 cheaper. The cost-benefit principle then asks whether the car's convenience is worth more than $550 to her (willingness to pay).
- **Nerida's MBA.** Inputs: $60,000 tuition, 2 years, current salary $70,000/yr.
  - Opportunity cost = $60,000 + 2 × $70,000 = **$200,000**.
  - Room and board are excluded because she pays them either way. This illustrates lesson 3: "Not all out-of-pocket costs are real opportunity costs."

### Inferences
- **Method to teach:**
  1. Compute the marginal product (Δoutput) of each added unit.
  2. Convert it to MB in dollars.
  3. Compute MC of that unit.
  4. Keep adding units while MB ≥ MC; stop where MB = MC (Rational Rule).
- **Fixed costs and sunk costs** (rent, owner's time) don't affect the *how many* decision. They matter only for the *either/or* decision of whether to open at all (cost-benefit principle), which is why S&W say apply the marginal principle first and then cost-benefit.
- **Including the owner's $1,000 opportunity cost** makes this *economic* profit. Accounting profit (ignoring it) would be $2,200 at 5–6 workers.
- **When MB = MC exactly, the decision maker is indifferent.** Total profit is the same at 5 or 6 workers. Expect a tricky MC question on this; the Rational Rule answer is "6 workers (where MB = MC)," and both give maximum surplus.
- **PPF exam math:**
  - opportunity cost of X = (units of Y given up) ÷ (units of X gained);
  - from the textbook example, 1 psychology point costs 2 econ points, and 1 econ point costs 0.5 psychology points;
  - a linear PPF means constant opportunity cost; a bowed-out PPF means increasing opportunity cost.
