**1.** *(Medium)* Kenji has a free Friday night. He ranks his options: (1) a jazz show with a $25 ticket, (2) a paid shift that earns $60, (3) studying. He goes to the show. What is his opportunity cost?

A) The $25 ticket only
B) The $60 shift and the time he could have spent studying
C) The $60 shift and the $25 ticket
D) The $60 shift only

**2.** *(Hard)* Priya is weighing a one-year master's program. Tuition and books cost $45,000, and a scholarship covers $10,000 of it. She would give up a $62,000 salary. Rent and food run $20,000 a year whether or not she enrolls. Enrolling also forces a $3,000 move she would not otherwise make. What is the opportunity cost of the program?

A) $97,000
B) $110,000
C) $100,000
D) $120,000

**3.** *(Hard)* You paid $80 for a nonrefundable concert ticket. On the day of the show, your manager offers you a shift paying $120 at the same time. You can resell the ticket online for $30. Attending the concert is worth $100 to you. What should you do?

A) Attend, because otherwise the $80 is wasted
B) Attend, because the concert's $100 benefit plus the $80 already paid exceeds the $120 wage
C) Work the shift and resell the ticket, which leaves you $50 better off than attending
D) Work the shift and resell the ticket, which leaves you $70 better off than attending

B) is applying the marginal principle
C) is correctly ignoring sunk costs
D) is showing a framing effect, because Plan A and Plan 1 describe identical outcomes

**5.** *(Hard)* You have 3 hours of study time. Each hour on economics adds 8 points to your econ grade, and each hour on psychology adds 4 points to your psych grade. A new technique doubles your psychology productivity to 8 points per hour and leaves economics unchanged. Which statement is true?

C) The PPF shifts outward in a parallel fashion.

**B1 · Marginal analysis (Chapter 1).** A coffee cart sells cups for $5. Ingredients cost $1.50 per cup, the barista earns $25 an hour, the daily permit is $60, and the owner's next-best use of the day is worth $100.

| Hours open | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total cups sold | 40 | 70 | 90 | 100 | 105 | 108 |

(a) Compute the marginal benefit and marginal cost of each hour.
(b) How many hours should the cart stay open? What is the economic surplus at that choice?
(c) If the owner's outside option rises to $200 a day, does the optimal number of hours change? Should the cart still operate?
(d) If the wage rises to $35 an hour, what happens to the optimal number of hours?

**B2 · Market equilibrium algebra (Chapter 4).** Demand is Qd = 200 − 10P and supply is Qs = 40 + 6P.

(a) Find equilibrium price and quantity.
(b) At P = $8, is there a shortage or a surplus? How large?
(c) An input price rise reduces supply by 16 units at every price. Find the new equilibrium.
(d) Starting from (c), demand also rises by 32 units at every price. Find the new equilibrium and say how price and quantity compare with part (a).

**B3 · Building market curves (Chapters 2–4).** A town has 200 type-A buyers, each with Qd = 6 − P, and 100 type-B buyers, each with Qd = 10 − 2P (zero when negative). There are 50 identical sellers, each with Qs = 2P.

(a) Write market demand for P ≤ $5 and market supply.
(b) Find the equilibrium price and quantity, and confirm your answer is in the range you assumed.
(c) Fifty new sellers enter. Find the new equilibrium. Which supply shifter is this?

## Explanations

**1. C.** The opportunity cost is your next-best alternative plus anything you pay to choose this option. His next-best use of the evening is the $60 shift, and the $25 ticket is money he could have spent elsewhere. Studying was third, so it doesn't count (trap B). This matches the slides' movie-night concept check.

**2. C. $100,000.** Net tuition is $45,000 − $10,000 = $35,000. Add the forgone $62,000 salary and the $3,000 move: $35,000 + $62,000 + $3,000 = $100,000. Rent and food are paid either way, so they aren't an opportunity cost (trap D adds them). Trap B forgets the scholarship, and trap A drops the move.

**3. C.** The $80 is sunk: you lose it whether you go or not. Compare only future-relevant options. Working and reselling gives $120 + $30 = $150. Attending gives $100 of enjoyment. Working wins by $50. Trap D subtracts the sunk $80 from one side.

**4. D.** Saving 2,000 of 6,000 jobs for certain is the same outcome as losing 4,000 for certain. Flipping from the sure option under "gains" language to the gamble under "losses" language is the framing effect from Tversky and Kahneman's experiments.

**5. A.** Before the change, one hour yields 8 econ points or 4 psych points, so 1 psych point costs 2 econ points. After the change, one hour yields 8 of either, so each point costs 1 of the other. Making psych cheaper raises the opportunity cost of econ, because every econ hour now gives up more psych. The PPF pivots out along the psych axis; it doesn't shift in parallel (C). 24 econ points use all 3 hours, which leaves zero psych points (D).

**6. B. 4 workers; $80.** Marginal benefit of a worker = $8 × extra loaves. Marginal cost = $150 wage + $2 × extra loaves.

| Worker | Extra loaves | MB | MC | Hire? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | 100 | $800 | $350 | Yes |
| 2nd | 80 | $640 | $310 | Yes |
| 3rd | 60 | $480 | $270 | Yes |
| 4th | 40 | $320 | $230 | Yes |
| 5th | 20 | $160 | $190 | **No** |

At 4 workers: revenue = 280 × $8 = $2,240. Costs = 280 × $2 + 4 × $150 + $400 + $600 = $2,160. Surplus = $80.

**7. C.** Rent is a fixed cost, so it doesn't change any marginal calculation: 4 workers is still the best number. It does change whether operating beats the next-best option. Surplus = $80 − $100 = −$20. Accounting profit (revenue minus explicit costs) is $2,240 − $1,660 = $580, but that ignores the $600 opportunity cost of her time (trap D). That gap is exactly why economists count opportunity costs.

**8. C.** A price change in the credit market (interest rates) changes your decision in the housing market. The slides use this exact example for dependence between markets.

**9. C. $7.** Buyer surplus = $11 − $7 = $4. Seller surplus = $7 − $4 = $3. Total = $7, which also equals willingness to pay minus marginal cost, $11 − $4. The price only splits the surplus; it doesn't create it.

**10. A.** Buy each gallon whose MB ≥ $2.25. Gallons 1–4 qualify ($5, $4, $3, $2.50), and the 5th ($2.00) doesn't. Surplus = (5 − 2.25) + (4 − 2.25) + (3 − 2.25) + (2.50 − 2.25) = 2.75 + 1.75 + 0.75 + 0.25 = $5.50. Trap C is total benefit ($14.50); surplus subtracts what you paid.

**11. D. 8.** Ava: 12 − 8 = 4. Ben: 8 − 4 = 4. Cal: 6 − 8 = −2, which becomes 0. Total = 8. Adding the −2 gives trap A. Always add quantities at a given price, never prices.

**12. C.** A change in a good's own price moves you along its demand curve (a change in quantity demanded). Coffee and cream are complements: with less coffee bought, demand for cream falls.

**13. D.** For an inferior good, higher income decreases demand. A cheaper substitute also decreases demand. Both push demand left, so the direction is clear. "It depends" (C) only applies when two forces push in opposite directions.

**14. B.** A congestion effect means a good becomes less valuable as more people use it. A is a network effect, C is a movement along a demand curve (an own-price change), and D is a preference/seasonal shift.

**15. B. 5 million.** Scale factor = 2,000,000 / 500 = 4,000. Market Qd = 1,250 × 4,000 = 5,000,000. Trap C multiplies 1,250 by the whole population.

**16. D.** Treat each coffee as a marginal decision. 1st: $6.00 ≥ $5.00, buy (surplus $1.00). 2nd: $4.50 ≥ $2.50, buy ($2.00). 3rd: $3.00 ≥ $2.50, buy ($0.50). 4th: $1.50 < $2.50, stop. Surplus = $3.50. Check: total benefit $13.50 − total paid $10.00 = $3.50. Trap C reports total benefit.

**17. B.** Expectations about future prices shift current demand. The 2025 tariff announcements triggered exactly this "front-loading" of imports.

**18. C.** Supply is the marginal cost curve, and a flat fee doesn't change the cost of producing one more loaf. In the long run a higher fixed cost could drive bakeries out of the market (a change in the number of sellers), but this week's supply curve doesn't move.

**19. A.** Produce while P ≥ MC: units 1 ($2), 2 ($3), and 3 ($5) qualify; unit 4 ($8 > $6) doesn't. Producer surplus = (6 − 2) + (6 − 3) + (6 − 5) = $8. Trap C is revenue.

**20. B.** The marginal cost of a wash = wage ÷ marginal product: $24/10 = $2.40, $24/8 = $3.00, $24/6 = $4.00, $24/4 = $6.00. Hire while MC ≤ $4.50, so 3 workers, producing 10 + 8 + 6 = 24 washes. Diminishing marginal product is what makes MC rise and the supply curve slope upward.

**21. D.** Corn and wheat are substitutes in production. When corn becomes more profitable, land shifts to corn, so wheat supply shifts left: wheat price up, wheat quantity down. In the corn market itself, the response is a movement along the supply curve.

**22. C.** More beef production means more hides. Leather supply shifts right, so its price falls and its quantity rises.

**23. B.** Each refinery supplies 5(3.60) + 5 = 23 million gallons. Times 100 refineries = 2,300 million = 2.3 billion gallons.

**24. D.** With a storable good, expecting a higher future price makes it worth holding output back. Current supply shifts left, which raises today's price. The same logic is why expected shortages raise prices immediately.

**25. A.** Set 120 − 4P = −30 + 6P, so 150 = 10P and P = $15. Q = 120 − 60 = 60.

**28. C.** A demand increase alone raises quantity, and a supply decrease alone lowers it. A higher price with unchanged quantity requires both shifts, with offsetting effects on quantity. This is the slides' diagnostic logic carried one step further.

**29. D.** Price and quantity moved in opposite directions, so supply must have shifted, and it must have increased. Demand may also have shifted (B is possible but not required).

**30. B.** The drought decreases supply (price ↑, quantity ↓). The study increases demand (price ↑, quantity ↑). Both raise price, and they pull quantity in opposite directions, so quantity is ambiguous.

**31. D.** A shortage pushes the price up. As the price rises, buyers and sellers move along their existing curves; nothing shifts. Trap A says demand and supply "fall" and "rise," which describes shifts and is the most common wording mistake on exams.

**32. B.** Both shifts push price down, so price fell for sure (D is wrong). They pull quantity in opposite directions: the demand decrease lowers it and the supply increase raises it. Because quantity rose, the supply shift dominated.
